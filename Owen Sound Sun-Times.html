Rural Ontario takes the global stage amid 75% projected surge in power demand

Ten years after the first nuclear industry gathering in Bruce County, the conversation is shifting to growth

By
Greg Cowan
September 13, 2026 at 11:07 a.m.
Last Updated: September 14, 2026 at 12:46 p.m.

In less than a month’s time, leaders in the nuclear energy sector will gather with community leaders and academics at the Nuclear Innovation Institute’s Clean Energy Frontier conference.

The gathering began 10 years ago and was then held by Bruce Power and Bruce County to spark collaboration and economic development opportunities amid support for the nuclear operator’s multi-component refurbishment project.

The MCR is underway, on budget, and in some cases ahead of schedule. It’s expected to be completed in 2033, and eyes are now looking toward the future.

The provincial and federal governments are banging the drum for more energy amid an increasingly electrified economy, the rise of artificial intelligence, the need for more homes, and electric vehicles creeping further into both public and corporate life.

In this context, the Bruce, Grey and Huron region will once again become the meeting point for Canada’s energy conversation.

On October 8 and 9, 200 attendees are expected to gather at the Unifor Family Education Centre in Saugeen Shores for the conference, built around the theme “It Starts Here.”

“The nuclear sector globally is watching our region,” said NII president and CEO Jessica Linthorne. “The world is watching nuclear right now, and to be in rural Ontario and on the global stage is very significant.”

In 2024, Ontario’s Independent Electricity System Operator (IESO) said in its annual forecast that Ontario’s energy demand will grow by 75 per cent by 2050.

“The provincial and federal government are very aligned and very supportive of nuclear. Projections for energy demand in Ontario are positioning nuclear as a requirement, because nuclear power is our baseload power,” said Linthorne.

Today, 30 per cent of Ontario’s power grid is supplied by the Bruce Power plant in Tiverton.

“We cannot become complacent. We need to continue to work together and create this opportunity. It’s not something to take for granted, and I think that’s a piece at our conference. We bring content forward that helps people understand,” said Linthorne.

The conference will include a keynote from Dan Riskin, biologist, science journalist, author and television host, on the power of scientific thinking to solve real-world problems, followed by two rounds of workshops covering investment attraction, provincial advocacy, municipal resilience and workforce pathways, featuring speakers such as Arveen Mann, investor services advisor, Americas, Invest Ontario; Joani Gerber, chief executive officer, investStratford; and Bill Walker, VP of government relations, BWXT and former Ontario Associate Minister of Energy.

“It’s a diverse audience, but all there with the same opportunity to work collaboratively through this growth. We have a tremendous opportunity in front of us with nuclear, and the proposed Bruce C site. That certainly will be a big topic of conversation,” Linthorne said.

On Day Two, there will be a full day of sessions on Canada’s economic and policy outlook, the state of nuclear refurbishment and power generation, national energy coordination, sector transformation, large-scale infrastructure execution and safety—and features speakers Robert Lavigne, executive vice president, economic research at OMERS and Eric Chassard, president and CEO at Bruce Power and more.

Linthorne said Canadian collaboration and Bruce Power’s commitment to spending 95 cents of every dollar domestically, with its robust Canadian supply chain, is something the industry takes pride in.

“That means Canadian jobs and energy security for us as a country. Which, yes, given the political landscape right now, we need to be, as Canadians, really mindful of how we can do these projects as Canadians and with Canadian materials,” Linthorne said.

A growing and successful offshoot of Ontario’s nuclear energy file is medical isotope production.

At Bruce Power, CANDU reactors and an isotope production system produce cobalt-60 and Lutetium-177, isotopes used for medical device sterilization, brain tumour treatment, and targeted prostate and neuroendocrine cancer therapy.

Ontario, a global leader in medical isotope production, aims to double its overall capacity by 2030 and earlier this year announced a $250 million investment in a production partnership between Bruce Power and the Saugeen Ojibway Nation.

“This is game-changing when we think about fighting cancer and sterilizing medical equipment,” Linthorne said. “I think 10 years from now we’re going to be talking a lot more about how CANDU reactors enable that.”

The NII is a member-based, not-for-profit organization that bills itself as Canada’s premier research institute for the clean energy sector.

“We’re in the business of saving time and money, and enhancing safety, as well as opportunities and training initiatives for staff,” Linthorne said. “So we’re driving that conversation in tandem with the conversation on economic development and economic opportunity.”

The conference in Bruce County began while the conversation in the sector was about fixing what was already there. Now, in 2026, the Clean Energy Conference is happening in a world where the nuclear energy sector, and the Canadian energy economy as a whole, is being tasked with keeping up with a growing demand.

“It’s our turn as Canadians and Ontarians to make big decisions and build so that future generations have what we have today,” Linthorne said.

For more information and to view the full agenda of the 2026 Clean Energy Conference, visit nii.ca/clean-energy-frontier-conference.
