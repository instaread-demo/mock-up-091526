Who Paid For Democracy?

Trying to check the referendum receipts

Sep 14, 2026

/

Politics
Words by

Elías Þórsson

The EU referendum is over; now it’s time for the hangover. And this nastiest of campaigns has exposed critical financial regulation oversight that the government failed to address.

On August 12th, less than three weeks before the vote, Minister of Justice Þorbjörg Sigríður Gunnlaugsdóttir was asked by RÚV about an The Organisation for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) report warning that the groups running both campaigns faced no donation limits or duty to disclose their finances. She replied: “This is how the rules are now, and then we’ll take a look at how we’ll be moving forward before the second referendum.”

Needless to say, there won’t be a second referendum, but it was telling that the government had either failed to recognise, or chose to ignore, this glaring oversight in campaign regulations. With allegations of misinformation being lobbed around, a review of unregulated referendum campaigning is vital to the health of the Republic.

No summer vacation

Icelanders are not used to summer political campaigns and with parliament on vacation and most of us heading to the cabins of the countryside, or the beaches of Tenerife, we tend to take a vacation from partisan politics. But this year was different, as our radios, our TVs, billboards and brochures stuffed through our mailboxes forced us to engage in a political debate about the EU.

All of that cost money. Quite how much, and whose, is anyone’s guess, and the crazy thing is: we’ll probably never know. When it comes to parliamentary, presidential and municipal elections, the legal framework is quite strict and robust. Political parties are not allowed to receive more than 550.000 ISK a year from any single donor, anonymous and foreign donations are banned, and anyone who gives more than 300.000 is named publicly. Individual candidates are only allowed to receive 400.000 ISK per donor.

While parties’ referendum spending falls under their ordinary annual reporting, the political finance law does not cover civil society groups, informal initiatives or other non-party actors, so no referendum-specific rules on donations, spending, disclosure or oversight applied to them. The National Audit Office gets no reports before voting day, cannot impose fines itself, and has no mandate over civil society groups. Most of the earlier finance recommendations from ODIHR, the OSCE’s democracy watchdog, remain unaddressed.

Furthermore, social media largely falls outside the Media Law’s campaign obligations. The EU Digital Services Act doesn’t apply in Iceland, so cooperation with the platforms is voluntary. Netvís, the state online-safety centre, detected coordinated automated activity during the last election but couldn’t trace its origin.

The OSCE raises the alarm

From July 14th to 17th, a two-person team from ODIHR, Lusine Badalyan and Ranko Vukčević, was in Iceland to meet with officials, parties, media, civil society, academics and diplomats. Everyone they spoke to trusted the administration to run the vote impartially and competently. Many, however, raised concern over gaps in the legal framework, campaign finance oversight and media regulation. On that basis the mission recommended sending a referendum expert team to look at the campaign and online campaigning, campaign finance and its oversight, and the media. This was rather striking: it was the first time since the 2017 parliamentary election that ODIHR had deemed an Icelandic vote worth sending experts to.

The report also noted that the referendum was taking place in a highly polarised environment and dealt with serious issues such as fisheries, the króna and the possible adoption of the euro. As RÚV reported, the concern was that wealthy interests, both foreign and domestic, might see benefit in influencing the outcome.

How much? Who knows?

The yes campaign SJÁ says it raised about 80 million ISK, while the no campaign Áfram Ísland says it spent just over 90 million. Neither will name its donors. The issue here is that we have no way of knowing if this self-reporting is accurate, or if it is, who is behind the funding. Áfram Ísland’s chairman, Halldór Benjamín Þorbergsson, said on RÚV that the campaigns’ accounts should have gone to the National Audit Office, but when asked what stood in the way of publishing Áfram Ísland’s own account, he cited donor confidentiality. In a Vísir debate in August, Snærós Sindradóttir, director of the pro-EU group Evrópuhreyfingin, estimated that Áfram Ísland was spending 200 to 300 million. However, these were not the only two players. Last December, before SJÁ and Áfram Ísland had even been founded, the government announced that Evrópuhreyfingin and the anti-EU group Heimssýn would each receive 10 million ISK.

The only hard numbers available come from Meta’s ad library, which shows that between June 1 and polling day the no campaign Áfram Ísland spent just over 3.9 million ISK on 570 Facebook and Instagram ads, while the yes campaign SJÁ spent just over 2.6 million on 821 ads. Those figures exclude ad production, billboards, broadcast ads, offices and nationwide meetings. They are the tip of an iceberg nobody is required to show us.

Foreign money? No way of knowing

Nothing in the law stopped campaign groups from accepting money from abroad, or from donors who wished to stay anonymous. Both SJÁ and Áfram Ísland said they took no foreign money, and nothing has surfaced to suggest otherwise. But with no disclosure requirement, their word is as far as anyone can check.

The authorities were watching. For months, the National Police Commissioner’s analysis unit monitored whether foreign states were trying to sway the result while sailing under a false flag. Finnbogi Jónasson, an assistant chief superintendent in the unit, told Vísir in August that police were examining whether money for campaign work was flowing between foreign governments and people in Iceland, whichever side made the approach. He would not say whether they had found anything.

Police also said they had seen new websites whose backers were unclear. One that drew attention, ríkissjóður.is (“the Treasury”), published claims about the cost of the króna. The finance minister said it had nothing to do with the state, and the site has since added a disclaimer saying so, without revealing who runs it.

ODIHR’s expert team was sent in part to assess how well Iceland’s institutions could identify and respond to disinformation and potential foreign and domestic interference. What no one could do was simply follow the money because the law never required the campaign groups to disclose it.

ODIHR’s final report is due in the coming months, and judging by what we know, it is likely to raise serious concerns about the rules governing the campaign. The problem, though, is that it won’t tell us how much was donated, who donated it, and why they decided to wade in.
